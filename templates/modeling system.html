The Basics of Modeling

Before a computer can control a physical machine, we have to translate that machine into numbers. This is called "Modeling". Think of it as writing a math recipe for reality.

The Physical World

Our pneumatic system pushes a metal rod (a piston) back and forth. To understand how it moves, we can imagine a simple real-world scenario: A heavy block attached to a rubber band, sliding on a sticky floor.

The Heavy Block = Mass (M)

Heavy things are hard to push and hard to stop once they start moving. This is inertia.

The Sticky Floor = Damper (D)

Friction slows things down. Imagine trying to move your hand through a pool of honey. That resistance is damping.

The Rubber Band = Spring (C)

A spring always wants to pull you back to the starting point. The further you stretch it, the harder it pulls back.

The Push = Air Force (F)

This is the compressed air pushing against the piston. Higher pressure means a harder push.

The Math Translation: If we add up the Push (F), subtract the Honey (D), and subtract the Rubber Band (C), we know exactly how fast the Heavy Block (M) will accelerate!

The Magic Formula (Transfer Function)

Engineers use something called a Transfer Function. It sounds scary, but it's just a black box. You put an input in (Air Pressure), and you get an output out (Movement).

Using some fancy math (called Laplace transforms), we turn our real-world block, spring, and honey into this recipe:

Output (Movement) divided by Input (Pressure) equals our Recipe:

\[ T(s) = \frac{X(s)}{P(s)} = \frac{A}{Ms^2 + Ds + C}\]

Don't worry about the s² and s. Just know that the bottom part of the fraction holds our Mass (M), Sticky Floor (D), and Rubber Band (C).
